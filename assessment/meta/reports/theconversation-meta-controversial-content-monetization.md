ABC News Verify documented that Facebook monetized Australian accounts linked to white nationalism, neo-Nazism, and anti-vaccine movements. Some of the monetized posts allegedly conflicted with Meta's own stated content policies.

Monetization program: Meta launched its Facebook content monetization program in 2024, combining previous features like ads on reels. The system operates on performance: "the more engagement your content receives, the more money Meta will pay you." In 2025, Facebook distributed nearly $3 billion to creators. The program remains invitation-only with undisclosed selection criteria.

How it works: the platform uses three separate layers — community standards (content removal), distribution systems (visibility), and monetization rules (payment). Content can remain online without being promoted or monetized, meaning the enforcement layers operate somewhat independently of each other.

The problem: research indicates inflammatory content performs disproportionately well. One study found "moral-emotional language was positively associated with sharing across multiple topics." Misinformation provokes more outrage than trustworthy reporting, facilitating wider distribution. When payments depend on engagement metrics, harmful content becomes financially incentivized unless policies consistently counteract this — which, per the reporting, they have not in these cases.

Accountability gap: the author notes that platforms "translate measurable attention into money, then rely on separate policy systems to prevent harmful material from benefiting" — a structural gap that allowed extremist-linked accounts to be monetized despite formally violating Meta's guidelines.
