OpenAI announced that its AI agents solved the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems worth $1 million. However, the achievement has been overshadowed by controversy regarding proper attribution.

The Core Dispute: NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster and Anthropic employee Levent Alpoge had worked on a simplified version of the problem for nearly a year using publicly available models. When they posted their proof on Mastodon, OpenAI simultaneously announced a solution to the full problem using an internal model.

According to Buckmaster's statement, OpenAI employees presented him with two options: publish independently and have OpenAI follow the next day, or collaborate on a paper excluding Alpoge due to his Anthropic affiliation. Buckmaster questioned whether OpenAI's agents accessed transcripts of his work, receiving denials about direct access but no response about whether models were trained on that material.

The Mathematical Approach: Both proofs employed a technique pioneered by mathematicians Diego Cordoba and Luis Martinez-Zoroa. While independent discovery remains possible, the identical methodological choice raises questions about influence.

Broader Implications: OpenAI solved the problem by running approximately 10,000 concurrent agents at a cost of millions of dollars -- resources unavailable to academic mathematicians. Prominent mathematician Terence Tao noted that "human-directed efforts to solve these problems tend to spur further development," warning that premature AI solutions could harm mathematical progress overall by eliminating opportunities for human insight and discovery.
