# navier-stokes-dispute

=== A string of controversies hits OpenAI, Anthropic | Fortune (https://fortune.com/2026/09/10/a-string-of-controversies-hits-openai-anthropic/) [independently scraped] ===

Two mathematicians who were working on the Navier-Stokes problem with Codex accused OpenAI of combing through their user logs to steal their work and solve the problem first. OpenAI denies this, but admits it doesn't know if the models trained on the mathematicians' work. "While unlikely, we cannot rule out that de-identified data derived from their usage of our products helped improve our models," OpenAI wrote in a post.

=== What OpenAI's latest controversy tells us about the future of math (https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/09/08/1143747/what-openais-latest-controversy-tells-us-about-the-future-of-math/) [independently scraped] ===

OpenAI's latest mathematical milestone has quickly become mired in controversy. OpenAI announced that its agents have solved the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems selected by the Clay Mathematics Institute in 2000 (a $1M prize problem; OpenAI says it will not claim the prize).

The announcement has been overshadowed by accusations that OpenAI used NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster's and Anthropic employee Levent Alpoge's AI-assisted work on the problem as a jumping-off point and failed to credit them. OpenAI has denied the accusations. Buckmaster and Alpoge had worked on a simplified version of the problem for almost a year using publicly available OpenAI and Anthropic models; when Buckmaster posted a partial proof on Mastodon, OpenAI announced a full solution the same day using an internal model.

According to Buckmaster, OpenAI employees presented him with two options: publish independently and have OpenAI follow the next day, or collaborate on a paper that excluded Alpoge from authorship due to his Anthropic affiliation. Buckmaster also wrote that he asked whether OpenAI's agents had accessed transcripts of his and Alpoge's work with OpenAI models -- which employees denied -- and whether OpenAI models had been trained on those transcripts, to which they gave no response.

Both proofs used an approach to the Navier-Stokes problem pioneered by mathematicians Diego Cordoba and Luis Martinez-Zoroa -- a coincidence that a Brown University mathematics professor, Javier Gomez-Serrano, says makes it "conceivable" (though not proven) that Buckmaster and Alpoge's work influenced OpenAI's. OpenAI's chief research officer, Mark Chen, again denied that any agents or employees accessed the transcripts, though the article notes that "given what has been revealed about the Hugging Face hack, it's clear that OpenAI is not always entirely aware of what its agents are doing."

OpenAI solved the full problem by running about 10,000 agents concurrently at a cost of millions of dollars -- resources unavailable to academic mathematicians. Prominent mathematician Terence Tao warned that AI labs "prematurely solving the problem... without full transparency into the solution process" could become "a net negative for the progress of mathematics as a whole," since it eliminates opportunities for human mathematical insight along the way.

No lawsuit or formal legal claim has been filed by Buckmaster or Alpoge; the dispute remains a public/reputational one, with OpenAI denying wrongdoing and no independent verification of whether transcripts were used.
